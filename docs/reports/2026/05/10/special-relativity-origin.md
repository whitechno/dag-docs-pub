# Summary: Lorentz, Poincaré, Einstein, and the Genesis of the Theory of Special Relativity

**Author:** Hector Giacomini (Institut Denis Poisson, Université d'Orléans –
Université de Tours – CNRS)
**Reference:** arXiv:2510.17838v2 [physics.hist-ph], 22 Oct 2025

## Central thesis

Giacomini offers a historical reading of how special relativity emerged, placing
the contributions of Lorentz, Poincaré, and Einstein inside their concrete
scientific and editorial context. His core claim is that **the true revolution
did not lie in special relativity itself, but in Maxwell's electrodynamics**.
Once electromagnetism made the speed of light a universal constant written into
the structure of physical law, special relativity became the practically
inevitable expression of an already transformed framework. The paper also gives
unusual weight to the German review journal *Beiblätter zu den Annalen der
Physik* as a channel through which Lorentz's results circulated quickly into the
German-speaking scientific community where Einstein worked.

## The setting: Bern and the *Beiblätter* (1905)

Einstein lived in Bern from 1902 to 1909 while employed at the Federal Patent
Office. Giacomini reconstructs the scientific infrastructure available to him:
the University Library (≈175,000 volumes in 1905, open evenings, with
interlibrary exchange across Switzerland), and especially the *Beiblätter zu den
Annalen der Physik* — a bibliographic supplement edited in Leipzig that compiled
critical reviews and listings of international scientific literature. Bern's
library carried the *Beiblätter* on the same footing as the *Annalen* itself.
Giacomini argues this journal was the principal route by which the
German-speaking world tracked foreign work in physics.

## Maxwell's electrodynamics as the real revolution

Maxwell (1861–1873), building on Ampère and Faraday, unified electricity,
magnetism, and light into one field theory. Two consequences shaped what
followed:

- the speed of light $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}$ is a universal constant
  fixed by the equations themselves, independent of any medium or observer;
- the field becomes an autonomous physical entity, ending action-at-a-distance
  physics.

This conflicts directly with Galilean velocity addition. Maxwell's framework
also became the prototype for the great twentieth-century field theories:
general relativity, quantum field theory, and gauge theories of the Standard
Model.

## The experimental crisis

- **Michelson–Morley (1881–1887):** repeated null results — no fringe shift, no
  detectable motion of the Earth through the ether. By the 1890s this null
  result was a central topic in journals across Germany, France, Britain, and
  the United States.
- **Trouton–Noble (1903):** no torque on a charged moving capacitor — a second
  non-detection of motion relative to the ether.

Physicists were forced to choose between modifying Newtonian mechanics or
modifying Maxwell's equations.

## Lorentz's contributions

- **1895 memoir:** introduced the first transformations to first order in $v/c$,
  and the auxiliary "local time" $t' = t - vx/c^2$. At this stage $t'$ was a
  purely mathematical device; absolute time tied to the ether was retained.
- **1904 memoir** (*Electromagnetic Phenomena in a System Moving with Any
  Velocity Less Than That of Light*, 135 pages, Amsterdam): under pressure from
  Poincaré's criticisms, Lorentz produced the **complete transformation**

  $$x' = l(v)\gamma(x-vt),\quad y' = l(v)y,\quad z' = l(v)z,\quad t' = l(v)\gamma\bigl(t - vx/c^2\bigr),$$

  with $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}$ and an undetermined scale factor $l(v)$. He
  postulated **length contraction** $L_\| = L_0/\gamma$ and showed that moving
  electromagnetic clocks run slower by a factor $\gamma/l(v)$. The combination
  explained the Michelson–Morley null result. Lorentz still kept the ether and
  the distinction between true time $t$ and local time $t'$.

A crucial bibliographic point Giacomini stresses: Lorentz's 1904 memoir was
widely diffused — translated into German for the *Encyklopädie der
mathematischen Wissenschaften* (May 1905), discussed by Wien, Abraham, Cohn,
Sommerfeld, and reviewed in February 1905 in the *Beiblätter* by **Richard Gans
**. Gans's one-page review concisely conveyed local time, length contraction,
clock slowing, and the explanation of the negative experiments to any
German-speaking reader. Most historians have ignored this review; Giacomini
argues it would have been "highly improbable" for any physicist following the
*Annalen* to overlook it.

## Poincaré's contributions, 1898–1906

- **1898 (*La mesure du temps*):** operational synchronization of distant clocks
  via light signals; simultaneity becomes a convention.
- **1900 (Paris Congress):** electrodynamic energy carries momentum; tensions
  between Maxwell and Galilean mechanics.
- **1902 (*La Science et l'hypothèse*, German translation 1904):** absolute
  space and time have no empirical meaning; states a *principle of relativity*.
- **1904 (St. Louis lecture, *The Present and Future of Mathematical Physics*):
  ** explicit, general statement: "The laws of physical phenomena must be the
  same for all observers in uniform translation." Published through five
  editorial channels including English in *The Monist* (Jan 1905) and discussed
  at Göttingen in early 1905 by Hilbert, Klein, Minkowski, and probably Born and
  Laue.
- **1905 (*Comptes Rendus*, 5 June):** stated the principle of relativity,
  proved that the **Lorentz transformations form a group**, fixed $l(v)=1$, and
  extended invariance of Maxwell's equations to nonzero charge and current
  densities.
- **1906 (Palermo memoir):** systematic treatment — group structure, **velocity
  addition law**, manifestly invariant electrodynamics, an implicit
  four-dimensional space-time, and integration of the **principle of least
  action** with relativistic invariance. Lorentz himself, in 1921, credited
  Poincaré as the one who first achieved "perfect invariance of the equations of
  electrodynamics" and explicitly formulated the *postulate of relativity*.
  Poincaré nevertheless retained the ether as a convenient (though dispensable)
  hypothesis and kept the *true time / local time* distinction.

## Einstein's path before 1905

Giacomini draws on Christian Bracco's research to document Einstein's reading
habits between 1899 and 1902:

- Frequent use of the **library of the Istituto Lombardo** in Milan (Palazzo
  Brera), which carried Lorentz, Poincaré, Planck, Wien, Drude, Boltzmann.
- Discovery there of the 1900 *Festschrift* for Lorentz's twenty-fifth doctoral
  anniversary, with contributions from most leading physicists of the time (
  including Lorentz's own paper extending his electron theory).
- An April 1901 letter asking Mileva Marić to mail Kirchhoff's treatise on heat
  from Zurich.
- A **December 1901 letter** to Mileva (largely ignored by historians) in which
  Einstein asked his former classmate Jakob Ehrat to send him "the latest works
  of Lorentz" — direct evidence that Einstein actively tracked Lorentz's
  electrodynamics years before 1905.

## Einstein's 1905 paper, "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies"

Einstein based his derivation on two postulates: the principle of relativity and
the constancy of the speed of light. Using homogeneity, isotropy, reciprocity,
light-cone invariance, and the requirement that two successive transformations
compose, he obtained the Lorentz form — formally identical to what Lorentz and
Poincaré had reached. He derived the **velocity addition law**

$$u' = \frac{u-v}{1 - uv/c^2}.$$

Two striking features are emphasized:

1. **Bibliographical silence.** The paper cites no contemporary work — not
   Michelson–Morley, not Trouton–Noble, not Lorentz 1904, not Poincaré. Einstein
   refers only to long-deceased figures (Newton, Maxwell, Hertz, Doppler) plus
   Lorentz as the heir of Maxwell. This silence contradicts the documented
   evidence that Einstein had been actively reading Lorentz since at least 1901.
2. **Title vs. content.** The title fits the existing tradition on the
   electrodynamics of moving bodies, but most of the paper is a kinematic
   reconstruction (clock synchronization and the Lorentz transformation), with
   electrodynamics confined to the end.

Einstein thanked **Michele Besso** at the close, recounting later (Kyoto, 1922)
that a mid-May 1905 conversation with Besso gave him the guiding insight:
Lorentz's $t'$ is not an auxiliary parameter but the time actually measured by
moving clocks.

## The Poincaré–Einstein chronology of 1905

Poincaré's brief *Comptes Rendus* note of 5 June 1905 was mailed around 9 June
and would plausibly have reached the Bern University Library by mid-June.
Einstein's relativity paper reached the *Annalen der Physik* on 30 June 1905.
Giacomini does not assert direct reading, but notes that Einstein had a roughly
two-week window in which the note was likely physically present in Bern, and
that the speed with which Einstein finalized the paper (alongside his other 1905
outputs — light quanta, Brownian motion, doctoral thesis, fourteen *Beiblätter*
reviews) suggests "a strong determination to publish without delay."

## Einstein's later attitude toward Poincaré

In nearly all later writings — Leiden 1920, Princeton 1921, Nobel lecture 1923 —
Einstein presented relativity as an adjustment to Maxwell–Lorentz
electrodynamics, citing Lorentz abundantly while **never mentioning Poincaré in
connection with relativity**. The single exception is a 1906 paper on the
inertia of energy, where Einstein acknowledged Poincaré's 1900 idea of
electromagnetic mass. Pais reported that in the 1950s Einstein claimed not to
know Poincaré's 1906 memoir; Helen Dukas confirmed it was not in his archives.
Only weeks before his death in 1955, Einstein asked Max Born to mention both
Lorentz and Poincaré in the Bern fiftieth-anniversary speech he was too ill to
deliver.

In his **1907 review article**, Einstein cited only four pre-1905 references:
Michelson–Morley (1887), Lorentz (1895, 1904), and Trouton–Noble (1903) —
explicitly acknowledging Lorentz's 1904 transformation while still ignoring
Poincaré's 1905 and 1906 papers. Strikingly, **neither Poincaré paper was ever
reviewed in the *Beiblätter***, despite other Poincaré works of the period being
summarized there — an editorial absence that contributed to Poincaré's
marginalization in the German-speaking reception.

## The "sudden stroke of genius" image

Lorentz (1892–1904) and Poincaré (1898–1906) left a continuous, published trail
of partial results. Einstein, by contrast, left no preparatory manuscripts: from
the spring of 1905 there are only scattered remarks in his correspondence, then
in late June a complete paper. Michel Paty: *"Einstein's work seems to have
appeared fully formed... The genesis of special relativity seems shrouded in
mystery."* This contrast nourished the "lightning-like act of genius" image (
Stachel, Norton, Holton).

## The "Lorentz–Einstein theory" and Poincaré's eclipse

Between 1907 and the 1920s, German physicists routinely spoke of the **"
Lorentz–Einstein theory"** (Planck 1907, Bucherer 1908, Levi-Civita 1910, Cohn
1910). The **1913 Teubner volume** *The Principle of Relativity*, edited by Otto
Blumenthal, collected Lorentz (1904), Einstein (1905), Einstein (1907), and
Minkowski (1908) — but no Poincaré. Minkowski's 1908 Cologne lecture *Space and
Time*, despite geometrizing ideas that Poincaré had already published, did not
mention him. The 1923 English (Methuen / later Dover) edition with Sommerfeld's
commentaries cemented the Anglophone narrative without Poincaré. In that same
1923 edition, Einstein inserted an unusual note declaring that Lorentz's "
detailed memoir... was not known to me at the time of writing" — eighteen years
after publication.

Two notable exceptions to the marginalization of Poincaré:

- **Wolfgang Pauli's 1921 encyclopedia article** (commissioned by Sommerfeld,
  written at age 21, becoming the definitive reference): Pauli credited Poincaré
  with the explicit formulation of the principle of relativity, the quadratic
  invariant, the group structure of the Lorentz transformations, and a geometric
  four-dimensional interpretation — restoring him as a true cofounder.
- **Feynman's *Lectures on Physics* (1965):** Feynman recalls that the principle
  of relativity was first stated and named by Poincaré.

## Logical equivalence of the two formulations

Giacomini's most pointed structural argument: Einstein's approach is often
described as *kinematic* and revolutionary, but it is no more kinematic than
Poincaré–Lorentz's. By **fixing the limiting speed at $c$ from the outset**,
Einstein implicitly imports Maxwell's electrodynamics into the kinematics. A
genuinely kinematic derivation would only postulate an invariant limiting
velocity (finite ⇒ relativistic, infinite ⇒ Galilean) and let experiment
identify it with the speed of light.

- For **Poincaré**, the constancy of $c$ is a *result* derived from the
  invariance of Maxwell's equations.
- For **Einstein**, Maxwell's invariance becomes a *theorem* derived from the
  constancy of $c$.

The two constructions yield identical structures (Lorentz transformations,
velocity addition, invariance of Maxwell's equations) and differ only by the *
*interchange of postulate and theorem**. Within Poincaré's framework, the ether
is dynamically unobservable, the local time coincides operationally with
measured time, and all observable quantities obey Lorentz invariance — his
theory is logically coherent and predictively equivalent to Einstein's, only
conceptually heavier.

## Conclusion

The historical impact of the postulate-vs-theorem inversion was enormous:
Einstein became the "founder by principles," while Poincaré was cast as a "
dynamical theorist" of the electron. Yet, Giacomini argues, both constructions
flow directly from Maxwell's electrodynamics. Special relativity did not arise
*ex nihilo*; it was the practically inevitable consequence of a coherent
electrodynamics already in place. In Einstein's own 1907 reasoning: if Maxwell's
equations retain their form in all inertial frames, then the value of $c$ they
imply must be identical for every observer — the principle of relativity plus
Maxwell's equations *is* special relativity. The revolution had already
occurred.
